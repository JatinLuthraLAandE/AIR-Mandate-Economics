📓 Complete Visual Notes: Fiscal Policy

1. Meaning & Types of Fiscal Policy[cite: 3, 4]

Fiscal Policy involves the government making deliberate attempts to adjust revenues, expenditures, and public debt to eliminate unemployment during recession and achieve price stability during inflation. It acts as a contra-cyclical tool[cite: 4].

a) Expansionary Fiscal Policy
  • Used during recessions or deflation to correct instability and close a 'recessionary gap'[cite: 4].
  • Stimulates the economy by boosting aggregate spending/demand[cite: 4].
  • Instruments: Increase government spending and/or decrease taxes[cite: 4].
  • Result: Often leads to a budget deficit because tax cuts reduce income while expenditures exceed revenues[cite: 4].
b) Contractionary Fiscal Policy
  • Used during an inflationary phase or asset bubble to eliminate an 'inflationary gap'[cite: 4].
  • Restrains the level of economic activity and curtails aggregate demand[cite: 4].
  • Instruments: Decrease government spending, increase personal income taxes (reduces disposable income), increase business taxes (reduces firm investments)[cite: 4].
  • Result: Ideally leads to a smaller deficit or a larger budget surplus[cite: 4].

2. Expenditure & Debt Instruments[cite: 3, 4]

Public expenditures are income-generating and include capital expenditures on public works, relief, subsidies, and transfer payments (spending that doesn't contribute to GDP directly, just transfers income)[cite: 3].

🏦 Public Debt as a Fiscal Instrument

A rational policy of public borrowing and debt repayment is a potent weapon to fight inflation and deflation[cite: 3].

  • Internal Debt: Borrowing from its own people/domestic market[cite: 3].
  • External Debt: Borrowing from outside sources/international agencies[cite: 3].
  • Forms: Takes two main forms namely, market loans (T-bills, bonds) and small savings[cite: 3].
  • For financing capital projects, long-term capital bonds are floated; for meeting short-term expenditure, treasury bills are issued[cite: 4].

3. The Crowding Out Effect & Conclusion[cite: 4]

⚠️ What is Crowding Out?

When the government aims to solve a recession with expansionary policy, it runs a budget deficit. This deficit is financed by borrowing from the credit market (issuing long-term bonds). This massive borrowing can push up interest rates, which causes private spending and investment to shrink (be "crowded out"). In such cases, fiscal policy impact is smaller than it should be, making it ineffective[cite: 4].

Exception: During deep recessions, crowding-out is less likely to happen because private sector investment is already minimal (insignificant private spending to crowd out), and the government can borrow without increasing interest rates[cite: 4].

Conclusion: Well-designed and timely fiscal responses are necessary. If an economy has idle capacity and unemployed workers (recession), an increase in aggregate demand increases output without changing prices. If an economy is at full employment, expansionary policy only pushes prices up (inflation) with no impact on total output[cite: 4].

⚡ Quick Revision Notes — Unit 4

  • • Objective: Eliminate unemployment (recession) and achieve price stability (inflation)[cite: 4].
  • • Expansionary Policy: Used in recession. ⬆️ Govt Spending, ⬇️ Taxes = Budget Deficit[cite: 4].
  • • Contractionary Policy: Used in inflation. ⬇️ Govt Spending, ⬆️ Taxes = Budget Surplus or smaller deficit[cite: 4].
  • • Transfer Payments: Govt spending that does not contribute to GDP directly (e.g., pensions)[cite: 3].
  • • Public Debt Types: Internal vs External. Takes the form of market loans and small savings[cite: 3].
  • • Crowding Out: Government deficit borrowing raises interest rates, substituting/displacing private sector investment[cite: 4].
  • • Deep Recession Exception: Crowding out is less likely during deep recessions as private investment is already minimal[cite: 4].
