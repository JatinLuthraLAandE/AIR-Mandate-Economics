Ch 2 Unit 3: Supply & Elasticity of Supply

Weightage: Core Micro Pillar (~2–3 Marks) | Stock vs Supply & Elasticity Geometry

1. Concept of Supply vs. Stock

Supply refers to the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer to the market at various prices during a given period of time.

  • Stock: The total quantity of a commodity available with the seller at any given point in time (a static/stock concept).
  • Supply: That part of the stock which the seller is actually ready to bring to the market at a specific price (a flow concept). Supply can be equal to or less than stock, but cannot exceed total available stock in the short run.

2. Determinants of Supply

Supply function: $S_x = f(P_x, P_f, P_r, T, G, N, E_p)$

  • Own Price ($P_x$): Direct relationship (ceteris paribus). Higher price yields higher profit margin $\implies$ higher supply.
  • Prices of Factors of Production ($P_f$): Inverse relationship. Rise in input costs (wages, raw materials) reduces profit margins $\implies$ supply falls.
  • Prices of Related Goods ($P_r$): If price of a substitute-in-production (e.g., wheat) rises, farmers shift resources away from rice $\implies$ supply of rice falls.
  • State of Technology ($T$): Technological improvements lower production costs $\implies$ supply increases.
  • Government Policy ($G$): Excise taxes and GST reduce supply (shifts curve left); subsidies increase supply (shifts curve right).
📌 Movement Along Supply Curve vs Shift in Supply Curve:
• Change in Quantity Supplied (Movement): Caused ONLY by a change in own price ($P_x$). Upward movement = Expansion of supply; Downward movement = Contraction of supply.
• Change in Supply (Shift): Caused by factors other than own price (technology, taxes, input costs). Rightward shift = Increase in supply; Leftward shift = Decrease in supply.

3. The Law of Supply & Exceptions

"Other things remaining constant (ceteris paribus), there is a direct/positive relationship between the price of a good and the quantity supplied." The supply curve slopes upwards from left to right.

Exceptions to the Law of Supply:

  1. Backward Bending Supply Curve of Labour: Beyond a certain wage rate, workers prefer leisure over work. As wage increases further, hours of labour supplied actually decrease.
  2. Agricultural Goods: Production depends on climatic conditions, rainfall, and seasons, not solely on price changes.
  3. Perishable Commodities: Sellers must dispose of goods (fruits, fish) even at lower prices to avoid total loss.
  4. Auction / Rare Goods: Supply of historical art (e.g., Mona Lisa) is fixed and completely vertical ($E_s = 0$).

4. Elasticity of Supply ($E_s$)

Measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price:

$$E_s = \frac{\% \Delta Q_s}{\% \Delta P} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \times \frac{P}{Q}$$

5. Geometric / Intercept Method of Supply Elasticity

For any straight-line linear supply curve extending backwards to touch the axes:

  • Passes through Origin: $E_s = 1$ (Unitary Elastic) regardless of the angle/slope!
  • Intercepts the Price (Vertical) Axis: $E_s > 1$ (Elastic supply).
  • Intercepts the Quantity (Horizontal) Axis: $E_s < 1$ (Inelastic supply).

6. Market Equilibrium

Determined at the intersection of Market Demand ($D$) and Market Supply ($S$): $Q_d = Q_s$.

  • If $P > P_e \implies$ Excess Supply (surplus) $\implies$ downward pressure on price.
  • If $P < P_e \implies$ Excess Demand (shortage) $\implies$ upward pressure on price.

🚨 ICAI Trap Zone & Mistake Tracker

Common Student Trap ICAI Exam Standard & Fact
A steeper linear supply curve passing through the origin has $E_s < 1$, while a flatter one has $E_s > 1$. False! ANY straight-line supply curve that originates from the origin has $E_s = 1$ everywhere, regardless of whether it is steep (e.g. 75°) or flat (e.g. 20°)! Elasticity depends on the origin intercept, not the slope alone.
Supply and stock can be used interchangeably in economics. False! Stock is the total volume available; supply is only the portion offered for sale at a specific price during a specific period. Supply can never exceed stock in the short run.
When a labourer's wage rate rises indefinitely, he will always supply more hours of labour. False! This creates a backward-bending supply curve. Beyond a threshold income level, the income effect (desire for leisure) dominates the substitution effect, causing hours supplied to fall as wages rise.

⏱️ 5-Minute Core Rules Summary

  • Law of Supply: Shows positive relation between price and quantity supplied (slope $= \Delta P / \Delta Q > 0$).
  • Arc Elasticity Formula for Supply: $E_s = \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{Q_2 + Q_1} \times \frac{P_2 + P_1}{P_2 - P_1}$.
  • Time Horizon Impact: Market Period (very short run) $\implies E_s$ is very inelastic / zero; Long run $\implies E_s$ is highly elastic because capacity can expand.
  • Indirect Taxes vs Subsidies: Taxes shift supply curve leftward/upward; subsidies shift supply curve rightward/downward.

🃏 3D Flip Flashcards

Mastered: 0 / 4
What is the elasticity of a straight-line supply curve passing through the origin?
$E_s = 1$ (Unitary Elastic) True for ANY straight line through the origin, regardless of steepness or slope!
Why does the supply curve of labour bend backwards?
Income Effect outweighs Substitution Effect! At very high wages, workers value leisure more than additional income, so hours of work supplied diminish.
How does time period affect price elasticity of supply?
Longer time = Greater elasticity ($E_s$). In the very short run (market period), supply is fixed ($E_s = 0$). In the long run, firms can adjust plant capacity and entry occurs ($E_s > 1$).
Difference between 'Increase in Supply' and 'Expansion of Supply'?
Expansion = Movement along curve due to rise in own price ($P_x$). Increase = Rightward shift of the entire curve due to non-price factors (technology, subsidies, lower input cost).

🎯 Unit 3 Practice MCQs

Score: 0

1. If a straight-line supply curve intercepts the positive Y-axis (Price axis), its price elasticity of supply is:

Correct! A straight-line supply curve cutting the price (vertical) axis has $E_s > 1$ (Elastic supply).

2. If price rises from ₹40 to ₹50 and quantity supplied increases from 100 units to 150 units, the price elasticity of supply (using percentage method) is:

Correct! $\% \Delta Q = \frac{50}{100} \times 100 = 50\%$. $\% \Delta P = \frac{10}{40} \times 100 = 25\%$. $E_s = \frac{50\%}{25\%} = 2.0$.
