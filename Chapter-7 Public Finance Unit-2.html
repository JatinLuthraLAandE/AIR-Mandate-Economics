Topic 2.1 & 2.2

Concept of Market Failure

⭐⭐⭐⭐ High Priority ⏱️ Ideal Study Time: 15 Mins

1. What is Market Failure?
Market failure occurs when the free market (driven by individual self-interest and price mechanisms) results in an inefficient allocation of society's scarce resources. This misallocation means there is either overproduction or underproduction of particular goods, leading to a less-than-optimal outcome for society.

🌗 Complete vs. Partial Market Failure

  • Complete Market Failure (Missing Markets): The market simply does not exist. The free market fails to supply any quantity of the good (e.g., National Defence).
  • Partial Market Failure: The market exists, but it produces the wrong quantity or at the wrong price (e.g., Over-producing polluting goods, under-producing healthcare).

⚠️ The 4 Major Sources of Market Failure

Why do free markets fail to achieve efficiency? Primarily due to:

  1. 👑 Market Power (Monopoly)
  2. 🏭 Externalities (Spillover Effects)
  3. 🛡️ Public Goods (Free Rider Problem)
  4. 🕵️ Incomplete Information (Asymmetric Info)
Topic 2.3

Market Power & Externalities

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Super High Priority (Always tested) ⏱️ Ideal Study Time: 25 Mins
👑 1. Market Power (Monopoly Power):

Firms with market power can profitably raise prices above Marginal Cost ($P > MC$). They deliberately restrict output to charge higher prices, leading to an under-allocation of resources and a deadweight loss to society.

🏭 2. Externalities (Spillover Effects)

Arise when the production or consumption of a good affects third parties who are not directly involved in the transaction. Prices fail to reflect true costs/benefits.

SOCIAL COST = PRIVATE COST + EXTERNAL COST
1. Negative Production Externality:
Social Cost > Private Cost. The market over-produces these goods. (e.g., Factory pollution).
2. Negative Consumption Externality:
Social Benefit < Private Benefit. Over-consumed by the market. (e.g., Smoking cigarettes, loud music).
3. Positive Production Externality:
Social Cost < Private Cost. The market under-produces these goods. (e.g., R&D, corporate training).
4. Positive Consumption Externality:
Social Benefit > Private Benefit. Under-consumed by the market. (e.g., Vaccinations, Education).
Topic 2.4 & 2.5

Public Goods & Information Failure

🛡️ Public Goods (Paul Samuelson, 1954)

Markets fail to supply pure public goods because private firms cannot force people to pay for them.

  • Non-Rivalry: One person's consumption does not reduce the amount available for others (e.g., breathing air, national defence).
  • Non-Excludability: Once provided, you cannot stop anyone from enjoying the benefit, even if they refuse to pay.
  • The Free-Rider Problem: Since people cannot be excluded, they have an incentive to consume the good without paying for it, leading to complete market failure.

🕵️ Incomplete Information (Asymmetric Info)

Occurs when buyers and sellers do not have equal access to information.

1. Adverse Selection (Akerlof's "Lemons"):
Occurs before the transaction. Bad products/risks drive good ones out of the market (e.g., used car market, unhealthy people buying health insurance).
2. Moral Hazard:
Occurs after the transaction. A party takes more risks because someone else bears the cost (e.g., driving recklessly after getting comprehensive car insurance).
Topic 2.6 to 2.13

Government Intervention & Government Failure

🛠️ Correcting Market Power & Externalities

  • Competition Act, 2002: India's law to prohibit anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominant position.
  • Pigouvian Taxes: Levied on polluters (negative externalities) equal to the marginal external cost. Makes them "internalize the externality".
  • Subsidies: Given for positive externalities (like education) to lower consumer prices and increase consumption.
  • Cap-and-Trade (Tradable Permits): Market-based approach allowing firms to buy/sell pollution permits.

⚖️ Price Controls (Floors & Ceilings)

  • Price Ceiling (Maximum Price): Set below the equilibrium price to protect consumers (e.g., rent control, essential medicines). Leads to shortages (black markets).
  • Price Floor (Minimum Price): Set above the equilibrium price to protect producers/workers (e.g., Minimum Support Price (MSP) for farmers, Minimum Wages). Leads to surpluses.

⚠️ Government Failure

Government intervention doesn't always result in an efficient allocation of resources. Government Failure occurs when the cost of government intervention exceeds the benefits, or when intervention creates fresh and more serious problems.

Causes: Information gaps by policymakers, bureaucratic red tape, corruption, and the deadweight loss of taxation required to fund the interventions.

15-Minute Rapid Revision

⚡ Concise Exam-Eve Revision Notes — Unit 2

1. Market Failure & Externalities

  • • Market Failure: Inefficient allocation of scarce resources.
  • • Monopoly Power: Firms restrict output and set $P > MC$, causing deadweight loss.
  • • Negative Externalities: Overproduction (Social Cost > Private Cost). E.g., Pollution.
  • • Positive Externalities: Underproduction (Social Benefit > Private Benefit). E.g., Education.

2. Public Goods & Info Failure

  • • Public Goods: Non-rival and Non-excludable. Suffer from the Free-Rider problem.
  • • Adverse Selection: Hidden info before a deal. (Lemons problem - bad drives out good).
  • • Moral Hazard: Hidden actions after a deal. (Taking risks because you have insurance).

3. Corrective Government Tools

  • • Pigouvian Tax: Imposed on negative externalities.
  • • Subsidies: Provided for positive externalities & merit goods.
  • • Price Ceiling: Maximum limit (protects consumers, causes shortages).
  • • Price Floor (MSP): Minimum limit (protects producers, causes surplus).

4. Government Failure

  • • Occurs when government intervention worsens resource allocation or creates new problems.
  • • Caused by information gaps, bureaucratic inefficiencies, and the deadweight cost of taxes.
