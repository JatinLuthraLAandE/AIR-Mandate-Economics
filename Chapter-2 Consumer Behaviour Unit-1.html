Ch 2 Unit 1: Law of Demand & Elasticity of Demand

Weightage: Core Micro Pillar (~5–6 Marks) | High Formula & Elasticity Density

1. The Meaning of Demand

In Economics, Demand is NOT just a mere desire. Effective demand requires three conditions:

  • Desire for the commodity.
  • Ability to pay (purchasing power).
  • Willingness to spend money to acquire it.

Demand is always expressed at a specific price and per unit of time (it is a flow concept).

2. Determinants of Demand

Quantity demanded depends on several factors: $Q_x = f(P_x, Y, P_r, T, A, E)$

  • Price of the commodity ($P_x$): Inverse relationship (ceteris paribus).
  • Income of consumer ($Y$):
    • Normal Goods: Direct relation (Income $\uparrow \implies$ Demand $\uparrow$).
    • Inferior Goods: Inverse relation (Income $\uparrow \implies$ Demand $\downarrow$).
  • Price of Related Goods ($P_r$):
    • Substitutes (Tea/Coffee): Direct relationship ($P_{tea} \uparrow \implies D_{coffee} \uparrow$).
    • Complements (Car/Petrol): Inverse relationship ($P_{car} \uparrow \implies D_{petrol} \downarrow$).
  • Tastes and Preferences: Includes modern psychological effects:
    • Bandwagon Effect: Buying because everyone else is doing so (imitation).
    • Snob Effect: Desiring exclusivity; demand falls when a good becomes common.
    • Veblen Effect (Conspicuous Consumption): Higher price makes it a prestige symbol (diamonds, luxury cars).
📌 Expansion/Contraction vs. Increase/Decrease:
• Change in Quantity Demanded: Caused ONLY by a change in own price ($P_x$). Results in movement along the same demand curve (Downward = Expansion; Upward = Contraction).
• Change in Demand: Caused by ANY factor OTHER than own price (income, tastes, related goods). Results in a shift of the demand curve (Rightward = Increase; Leftward = Decrease).

3. Rationale Behind the Law of Demand

Why does the demand curve slope downwards from left to right?

  1. Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility (Marshall): Consumer pays only what corresponds to the marginal utility; since $MU$ falls with each unit, more will be bought only at a lower price.
  2. Substitution Effect (Hicks & Allen): When price falls, the commodity becomes relatively cheaper than substitutes; consumers substitute it in place of other goods.
  3. Income Effect: Fall in price increases the real income (purchasing power) of the buyer, enabling higher purchases.
  4. Arrival of New Consumers: Lower price attracts consumers who couldn't afford it earlier.
  5. Different / Multiple Uses: When price falls, the good is diverted to less urgent uses (e.g., electricity, milk).

4. Elasticity of Demand & Calculation Methods

Elasticity measures responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in determinants.

A. Price Elasticity ($E_p$): $E_p = \frac{\% \Delta Q}{\% \Delta P}$

  • Arc Elasticity (Midpoint Method): Used when price change is large: $$E_p = \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{Q_2 + Q_1} \times \frac{P_2 + P_1}{P_2 - P_1}$$
  • Point Elasticity (Geometric Method): $E_p = \frac{\text{Lower Segment}}{\text{Upper Segment}}$ on a linear demand curve.
    • Midpoint: $E_p = 1$ | Upper half: $E_p > 1$ | Vertical axis intercept: $E_p = \infty$ | Lower half: $E_p < 1$ | Horizontal axis intercept: $E_p = 0$.
  • Total Outlay / Expenditure Method (Marshall):
    • Price & Total Outlay move in opposite directions $\implies E_p > 1$ (Elastic).
    • Total Outlay remains unchanged when price changes $\implies E_p = 1$ (Unitary).
    • Price & Total Outlay move in the same direction $\implies E_p < 1$ (Inelastic).

B. Income Elasticity ($E_y$):

  • $E_y > 0$ (Positive) $\implies$ Normal Goods.
  • $E_y > 1$ $\implies$ Luxury goods.
  • $0 < E_y < 1$ $\implies$ Necessities.
  • $E_y < 0$ (Negative) $\implies$ Inferior Goods.

C. Cross Elasticity ($E_c$):

  • $E_c > 0$ (Positive) $\implies$ Substitute goods (Slope of cross-demand curve is upward).
  • $E_c < 0$ (Negative) $\implies$ Complementary goods (Slope is downward).
  • $E_c = 0$ $\implies$ Unrelated goods.

🚨 ICAI Trap Zone & Mistake Tracker

Common Student Mistake ICAI Exam Standard & Fact
All inferior goods are Giffen goods. False. All Giffen goods are inferior, but not all inferior goods are Giffen! Giffen goods are a special subset where the negative income effect completely overwhelms the substitution effect, causing demand to rise when price rises.
If Total Expenditure increases when price increases, demand is elastic. False. If Price $\uparrow$ and Outlay $\uparrow$, they move in the SAME direction, which means demand is Inelastic ($E_p < 1$). Demand is Elastic only when Price and Outlay move in opposite directions.
Cross elasticity for complementary goods is positive because both are bought together. False. Cross elasticity for complements is NEGATIVE. When the price of cars rises, demand for petrol decreases. Only substitutes have positive cross elasticity.

⏱️ 5-Minute High-Yield Flash Points

  • Slope vs. Elasticity: Slope of linear demand curve is constant ($-\Delta P/\Delta Q$), but elasticity varies from $\infty$ to $0$ along the curve!
  • Rectangular Hyperbola Demand Curve: Has constant unitary elasticity ($E_p = 1$) at all points; total outlay is constant.
  • Arc Formula: Always use the Midpoint method when given two distinct price-quantity coordinates without specifying point derivative.
  • Giffen Paradox: Named after Sir Robert Giffen (Scottish economist studying bread/meat consumption among British workers).

🃏 3D Flip Flashcards

Mastered: 0 / 4
What is the substitution effect vs income effect of a price fall?
Substitution Effect: Good becomes relatively cheaper than rivals $\implies$ buy more. Income Effect: Real purchasing power rises $\implies$ buy more.
What is a Veblen good? How does it affect the demand curve?
Conspicuous consumption goods whose utility is derived from their high price and prestige. Exception to Law of Demand: Slopes upward!
What value does Point Elasticity take at the vertical and horizontal axes?
Vertical Axis (Price intercept): $E_p = \infty$ (Infinite) Horizontal Axis (Qty intercept): $E_p = 0$ (Zero)
Define the Snob Effect vs Bandwagon Effect.
Bandwagon: Demand rises as more people buy it (herd mentality). Snob: Demand falls as the good becomes common (desire for exclusive rarity).
