Chapter 6: Business Cycles

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Est. Study Time: 45 mins

1. Introduction & Meaning

Economics is concerned with fluctuations in economic activities. The economic history of nearly all countries point towards the fact that they have gone through periods of prosperity alternating with periods of economic downturns.

These rhythmic fluctuations in aggregate economic activity that an economy experiences over a period of time are called business cycles or trade cycles.

Characteristics: They occur periodically, but they do NOT exhibit the same regularity (they are not of the same length or intensity).

2. Phases of Business Cycle

A typical business cycle has four distinct phases:

  1. Expansion (Boom / Upswing): Characterized by increase in national output, employment, aggregate demand, sales, profits, rising stock prices. Continues till there is full employment of resources. Involuntary unemployment is almost zero (only frictional/structural remains).
  2. Peak (Boom / Prosperity): The highest point. Inputs become short in demand -> input prices increase -> output prices rise. Actual demand stagnates. The point where economic growth stabilizes before reversing.
  3. Contraction (Downswing / Recession): Increase in demand halts. Producers maintain investment leading to mismatch (Supply > Demand). Stoppage of orders, cancellation of investments. Incomes decline, demand falls, prices fall. Bank credit shrinks.
  4. Trough (Depression): Severe form of recession. Extremely sluggish economic activity. Negative growth. Prices are at lowest. Mounting unemployment. Fall in interest rate, but demand for credit is still low due to low investor confidence.

Recovery: Starts from the trough. Labour accepts lower wages -> lower costs -> business confidence revives -> investments pick up -> aggregate demand increases.

3. Economic Indicators

Used to measure and predict where the economy is headed.

  • Leading Indicators: Change before the real output changes. Signal the onset of cycles. Examples: Changes in stock prices, new orders for capital/consumer goods, building permits, money growth rate. (Note: They are not always 100% accurate!)
  • Lagging Indicators: Change after real output changes. Confirm the trends. Examples: Unemployment, corporate profit, labour cost per unit of output, commercial lending activity.
  • Coincident / Concurrent Indicators: Change simultaneously with the cycle. Show current state. Examples: GDP, Industrial production, inflation, retail sales, personal income.
📌 Famous Economists & Their Causes:
1. Keynes: Fluctuations in Aggregate Effective Demand.
2. Hawtrey: Purely monetary phenomenon (Money supply).
3. Pigou: Psychological factors (waves of optimism/pessimism).
4. Schumpeter: Innovations.
5. Nicholas Kaldor: Cobweb theory (present prices influence future production).

4. Causes of Business Cycles

A. Internal Causes (Endogenous):

  • Fluctuations in Effective Demand (Keynes)
  • Fluctuations in Investment (Prime cause per some economists)
  • Variations in government spending
  • Macroeconomic policies (Expansionary vs Anti-inflationary)
  • Money Supply (Hawtrey)
  • Psychological factors (Pigou)

B. External Causes (Exogenous):

  • Wars (diverts resources to weapons, affects consumer goods)
  • Post War Reconstruction (pushes up effective demand)
  • Technology Shocks (adoption of new tech boosts economy)
  • Natural Factors (weather/agriculture failures drag down industry)
  • Population growth (if > economic growth -> lesser savings -> low investment)

5. Relevance in Business Decision Making

Businesses must alter production relative to demand. "Cyclical" businesses (fashion, home builders, restaurants) suffer sharp drops during slumps and strong demand during booms. Non-cyclical/essential businesses are less affected. Some businesses even benefit from downturns (e.g., cheaper alternative brands).

🚨 ICAI Trap Zone & Mistake Tracker

ICAI loves to twist these specific concepts to confuse students in MCQs. Review these carefully!

What You Might Think (The Trap) What ICAI Actually Wants (The Truth)
Business cycles occur at regular, predictable intervals of time. False. They are recurrent and periodic, but they do NOT exhibit the same regularity (intensity and length vary).
Leading indicators are always accurate in predicting turning points. False. Leading indicators are not always accurate. Even experts disagree on their timing.
A.C. Pigou argued that trade cycles are a monetary phenomenon. False. Pigou = Psychological (Optimism/Pessimism). Hawtrey = Monetary.
Agriculture is the sector most prone to adverse effects of trade cycles. False. The Industrial sector (specifically capital goods and durable consumer goods) is disproportionately affected compared to agriculture.
During a depression, interest rates go up because money is tight. False. A typical feature of depression is the fall in the interest rate (though demand for credit still declines due to low investor confidence).

⏱️ 15-Minute Rapid Review Bullet Points

  • Business Cycles: Rhythmic fluctuations in aggregate economic activity. Occur periodically but not regularly.
  • Phases: Expansion (Boom) -> Peak -> Contraction (Recession) -> Trough (Depression).
  • Peak: Inputs fall short -> costs rise -> prices rise -> demand stagnates.
  • Trough: Negative growth, prices at lowest, high unemployment, low interest rates.
  • Recovery: Pervasive unemployment forces workers to accept lower wages -> anticipating lower costs, producers invest -> confidence takes off.
  • Leading Indicators: Signal onset. E.g., Stock prices, new orders.
  • Lagging Indicators: Confirm trends. E.g., Unemployment, corporate profits, labour cost per unit.
  • Coincident Indicators: Occur simultaneously. E.g., GDP, inflation, retail sales.
  • Schumpeter: Innovations cause cycles.
  • Nicholas Kaldor: Cobweb theory (present prices dictate future production).
