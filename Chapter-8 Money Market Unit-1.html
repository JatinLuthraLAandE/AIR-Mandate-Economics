Ch 8 Unit 1: The Concept of Money Demand

Weightage: Core Monetary Theory (~3–4 Marks) | Fisher, Cambridge, Keynes & Friedman

1. Nature of Demand for Money

Demand for money does NOT mean the desire to be wealthy. It refers to the demand to hold liquid cash balances (currency + demand deposits) rather than earning assets like bonds or equities.

It is a derived demand—money is demanded not for its own sake, but for the commands over real goods and services it provides (holding real cash balances $M/P$).

2. Classical Approach: Fisher's Quantity Theory of Money (1911)

Irving Fisher presented the Equation of Exchange in his book 'The Purchasing Power of Money':

$$MV = PT \quad \text{or with bank credit:} \quad MV + M'V' = PT$$

  • $M$ = Currency; $V$ = Transaction velocity of currency; $M'$ = Bank credit; $V'$ = Velocity of bank deposits; $P$ = General price level; $T$ = Volume of transactions.
  • Classical Assumptions: $V$ and $T$ are assumed constant in the short run (due to institutional habits and full employment).
  • Core Implication: There is a direct and strictly proportional relationship between the quantity of money ($M$) and the general price level ($P$). Doubling $M$ exactly doubles $P$, cutting the value of money ($1/P$) by half.

3. Neo-Classical / Cambridge Cash Balance Approach

Developed by Alfred Marshall, A.C. Pigou, D.H. Robertson, and J.M. Keynes. Emphasized money as a store of value, asking what fraction of income people choose to keep as cash balances:

$$M^d = k \cdot P \cdot Y$$

  • $Y$ = Real national income; $P$ = Price level ($P \cdot Y$ = Nominal Income); $k$ = Cambridge constant (fraction of nominal income people desire to hold as cash).
  • Crucial Link to Fisher: Mathematically, $k = 1/V$. The Cambridge $k$ is the exact reciprocal of Fisher's income velocity of money!

4. Keynes' Liquidity Preference Theory (1936)

In his General Theory, Keynes identified three distinct motives for holding cash balances:

  1. Transactions Motive ($L_1$): For day-to-day personal and business transactions. Directly proportional to income level: $L_1 = f(Y)$.
  2. Precautionary Motive ($L_1$): To provide for unexpected contingencies and emergencies. Function of income: $L_1 = f(Y)$.
  3. Speculative Motive ($L_2$): Money held to take advantage of market movements and fluctuations in bond prices.
    • Bond prices and interest rates are inversely related: $\text{Bond Price} = \frac{\text{Coupon Return}}{\text{Market Interest Rate}}$.
    • When interest rate is very high, bond prices are low $\implies$ expectation that interest rate will fall and bond prices will rise $\implies$ buy bonds $\implies$ Speculative demand for cash is LOW.
    • When interest rate is very low, bond prices are high $\implies$ expectation of future capital loss on bonds $\implies$ hold cash $\implies$ Speculative demand for cash is HIGH ($L_2 = f(r)$, negative slope).
📌 The Keynesian Liquidity Trap:
At a very low critical floor rate of interest ($r_{\text{min}}$), the speculative demand for money becomes perfectly elastic (horizontal). People expect interest rates can only rise from here (causing capital losses on bonds), so they hoard all additional money in cash. Here, monetary policy becomes completely impotent!

5. Post-Keynesian Developments & Milton Friedman

  • Baumol-Tobin Inventory Theoretic Approach: William Baumol treated transaction demand as an inventory holding problem. Showed that transaction demand is also interest-elastic!
  • Tobin's Portfolio Balance Approach: James Tobin explained that rational investors do not hold either all bonds or all cash (Keynes' assumption), but diversify between cash and bonds based on risk aversion.
  • Milton Friedman's Modern Quantity Theory: Money demand is a demand for a capital asset. It is determined by permanent income, wealth, returns on bonds ($r_b$), equities ($r_e$), physical goods ($dP/P$), and utility tastes. Friedman argued money demand is highly stable.

🚨 ICAI Trap Zone & Mistake Tracker

Common Student Error ICAI Standard & Fact
Bond prices and market interest rates move in the same direction. Never! Bond prices and interest rates are strictly INVERSELY related. When market interest rates rise, existing fixed-coupon bonds lose market value; when interest rates fall, bond prices surge.
In a liquidity trap, monetary policy is highly effective in stimulating investment. False! In a liquidity trap, the demand for speculative cash is perfectly elastic ($e=\infty$). Any money injected by the central bank is completely absorbed into idle hoards without lowering interest rates. Monetary policy becomes totally ineffective!
Keynes believed transaction demand for money depends heavily on interest rates. False! Keynes treated transaction and precautionary demands ($L_1$) as solely income-dependent ($L_1 = f(Y)$). It was William Baumol and James Tobin who proved transactions demand is interest-sensitive.

🃏 3D Flip Flashcards

Mastered: 0 / 4
What is the Keynesian Liquidity Trap?
A state where speculative money demand is perfectly elastic ($e = \infty$). Interest rates hit rock-bottom; investors hold all additional money as idle cash expecting bond prices to fall. Monetary expansion fails.
What is Cambridge 'k' and how is it related to Fisher's velocity 'V'?
$k = 1 / V$ (Exact Reciprocal) $k$ is the fraction of nominal annual income people desire to hold in liquid cash.
Why does speculative demand for money slope downwards with interest rates?
Inverse relationship between bond prices and interest rates! High interest rate means low bond prices $\implies$ capital gain expected $\implies$ money parked in bonds, not cash.
How does Milton Friedman define Permanent Income?
The expected long-term average stream of income! Friedman argued money demand does not fluctuate with temporary income blips, but depends stably on permanent wealth.
